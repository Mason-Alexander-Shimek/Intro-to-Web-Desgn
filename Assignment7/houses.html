The Wealthy Families Of Aldenmere

The nine houses

Fairchild Family

Upper Aldenmere, Fairchild House, a large late-Georgian property notable for the owl motif that appears consistently across its architectural details, carved into the gateposts, rendered in the ironwork of the balcony, present in the family's monogram. They are the family's most consistent personality trait, expressed in stone.

The Fairchilds are the most prominent of Fairbourne's bastard offshoot branches, descendants of a liaison between the third Fairbourne monarch's younger brother and a Cerani merchant's daughter in the mid-1600s, legitimised by a patent of nobility that is slightly more complicated to explain than the family's social position requires, and which the family accordingly does not explain unless directly asked. They have been in Aldenmere for a century and a half, and they have been collecting owls for most of it.

Fairchild House has a number of facilities housing over forty owls, actual pets of the family's members current patriarch, Cornelius Fairchild, fifty-eight, who believes that owls are the intellectually most honest animals. Predators who make no pretence of being anything else, and who are more perceptive than their stillness suggests. This is, those who know Cornelius observe, a reasonable description of the man himself.

His daughter Adeline Fairchild (see: First Ladies of Vallam) is the family member most visible in the city's social life. His son and heir Edmund Fairchild, thirty-one, manages the family's various investment interests from an office in Millfield and is widely considered to be quietly becoming more significant than his present profile suggests.

Harwick-Sel Family

Mid-Aldenmere, Harwick Hall, a Victorian Gothic property whose tower is the most visible silhouette on Aldenmere's slope after the Golf Course.

The Harwick-Sels are a merged family line produced by the marriage in 1876 of the Harwick banking family and the Sel shipping concern, explicitly arranged by both families' patriarchs for commercial reasons and has produced, in subsequent generations, both genuine affection and excellent return on investment. The family's current head is Lyra Harwick-Sel, fifty-nine, who inherited the combined enterprise after the deaths of both her husband and her father within eighteen months of each other in 1907, made a practical decision to manage both concerns as a single entity, and has spent the seventeen years since demonstrating that this was the right decision. She sits on the Vallam Chamber of Commerce board, the University of Vallam Court, and the advisory board of the Dominion Naval Command, whose last position is entirely appropriate given that the Harwick-Sel shipping concern holds three current naval supply contracts.

Calloway Family

Lower Aldenmere, Calloway Terrace, a row of four adjoining townhouses purchased by the family across three generations and opened into a continuous residence without obvious exterior alteration

The Calloways are a legal family. Four consecutive generations of barristers, solicitors, and at least one judge, whose current representative, Sir Gerald Calloway, sixty-two, sits on the Dominion Appeals Court and whose connection to Vallam's Masonic Lodge is well-known to its membership and politely unacknowledged outside it. The family's more interesting current member is his niece, Helena Calloway, twenty-nine, who graduated from the University of Vallam Law Faculty in 1919, one of the first batch of women to complete the degree, and has spent the six years since building a practice that specialises in cases that other firms decline. She is effective, her fees are not modest, and she has a client list that includes several individuals whose presence in a respectable law practice requires explanation that she does not provide.

Spencer House

Upper Aldenmere, Spencer House, a broad stone property built to communicate permanence.

The Spencers are industrialists. Not the old-money kind who made their fortune three generations ago and have since been living on the principal, but the working kind who are still making it and are not entirely finished. Lucas Spencer, fifty-five, is the family's current head: a large, physically imposing man who started in his father's iron foundry at fourteen and now owns, through the Spencer Industrial Group, the majority of the mining and timber operations in the regions surrounding Vallam and several further afield, along with a significant portfolio of freight facilities at the Millfield Yards. He does not speak loudly. He speaks once, at a volume appropriate to the room, and expects not to need to repeat himself. This expectation is met in most professional contexts.

His heir, Peter Spencer, twenty-two, returned from the Corda front one year ago. He had volunteered in 1922, served as an artillery officer, been decorated twice, and come back in a condition that anyone who has seen the front would describe without surprise and that Peter himself describes as fine. He is managing a return to the family business's management structure with the specific difficulty of a person who left for the war, spent four years in it, and is now expected to resume a trajectory that was designed for a different person. Lucas Spencer watches this process with a concern he does not know how to express to his son and a patience he is working at, because it does not come naturally.

The Rajiv Family

The Old Strand, Rajiv House. A shadowed, angular building of considerable age that sits at the Old Strand's northern extreme, overlooking the water on three sides. It has a quality of presence that people who pass it on the coastal path tend to feel before they can see it clearly.

The Rajivs are the second-oldest continuous family presence in Vallam, their history in the city predating the Fairbourne dynasty by at least two centuries according to records that the family has maintained and that no external archive fully corroborates. Their origins are in the Cerani-Răvin cultural borderlands, a heritage visible in the family's architecture and the character of their internal culture, which is warm within family lines and precisely calibrated at the boundary.

The family's most discussed aspect is its genetics, present with intensely bright violet eyes and white hair, present from childhood, unconnected to albinism and unexplained by any medical account that the Rajiv family has sought. This trait, which the family calls the clear in their own informal language, a word whose original meaning is not exactly clear (the irony is not lost), appears in perhaps one in three family members, and has done so for as long as the family's own records go back.

The current family head is Fabian Rajiv, seventy-two, who has violet eyes and hair the colour of new snow, and who conducts himself with patience, and knows what is in most rooms before he enters them. He has married children into most of the significant Aldenmere families over the last forty years, with what those who noticed describe as systematic care.

Foulere House

Upper Aldenmere, Foulere House, a broad, red-brick property built with the specific confidence of a family that arrived after the old money and decided this was not a disadvantage. The gateposts are topped with cast-iron anchors. This is not subtle, and it is not meant to be.

The Fouleres are three generations removed from the docks of the Reach's northern port towns, where the family's original fortune was made in commercial sea trade before Cathair Foulere's grandfather made the pivot to armaments manufacturing that the family has never looked back from. The Foulere Industrial Works, headquartered at a facility in the outer shires east of Vallam, produces a significant share of the Dominion's small-arms ammunition and naval ordnance, a line of business that has become, since 1903, the closest thing the family possesses to a printing press. The Great War has been very good to the Fouleres. They will not say this aloud, they are not unaware of what it implies about them, and they have made their private accommodations with both facts.

The family has a long standing friendly rivalry with the Spencers. The two patriarchs have been to each other's tables, have spoken at the same Chamber of Commerce events, and have, on a couple documented occasions, collaborated on logistics problems to mutual benefit. The Spencers hold the inland resource extraction, and the Fouleres hold the coastal and manufactured goods, and the boundary between those domains has been tested, pushed at, and held through about a decade of courteous hostility. The current patriarch, Cathair Foulere, fifty-one, is an immediately recognisable figure in Aldenmere’s culture. A large man with an extraordinary mane of copper-red hair that has not significantly greyed. He is loud, gregarious, and his handshake is bone-crushing. His son Riordan Foulere, twenty-eight, manages the sea trade arm of the business from the Merchant District offices and is considerably less fun at dinner.

Marie Family

A townhouse in lower Aldenmere proper, modest by the street's standards, supplemented by a significant collection of estate properties across Vallam's surrounding shires. The family's true holdings are in the rolling country south and west of Vallam.

The Maries built their Aldenmere position on wine, producing an estate wine operation whose quality became, over two generations, the kind of reputation that opens doors in Aldenmere without requiring explanation. The family was comfortably wealthy, socially secure, and entirely unprepared for National Prohibition, which removed the market for their primary product at approximately the same speed that the Great War removed their best remaining export routes to Corda's occupied territories.

Sylvia Marie, thirty-six, took over the family's management five years ago and has spent most of those five years in the process of becoming something more than a vintner's heir with a portfolio problem.

She is diversifying into hospitality with medical goods manufacturing, as well as ownership of many of Vallam’s clinics, including the central hospital. She is stunningly attractive, and the eldest sibling of the Rajiv's current mainline generation, the product of an earlier and differently-named branch of the family. She possesses none of the violet-eyed clarity that marks that bloodline's more visible members.

Langford Family

Mid-upper Aldenmere, Langford Court, a Georgian property of considerable size whose walls are notable for being the most well-maintained exterior on the entire upper slope.

The Langfords are the second significant bastard offshoot of the Fairbourne dynasty in Aldenmere, their line descending from a liaison between the fifth Fairbourne monarch's second son and a Vallam-born parliamentary administrator's daughter in the early eighteenth century, a generation later than the Fairchilds, and a legitimisation that came through a different legal mechanism, which the Langfords and Fairchilds have found no occasion to discuss in their two centuries of proximity. The Langford connection to the main Fairbourne line is sufficiently close and sufficiently documented that the family has, unlike the Fairchilds, held appointed government positions in most generations with advisory roles, departmental chairs, the occasional junior ministry. In 1926, the family's government connections are the most significant thing about them, and they know it, and the two things they have never done are ask for formal recognition of their lineage and behave as though they are not aware of it.

The Langford name is a household one in Vallam primarily through two channels: the Langford Foundation, a charitable body that funds scholarships at both universities, maintains three public reading rooms in the working-class districts, and operates a veterans' support program that is the city's most functional privately-funded equivalent; and Langford & Associates, the city's largest and most politically connected law firm. The current family head, Sir Emery Langford, sixty-six, holds a government advisory position in the Home Ministry that is described in official documents as consultative and functions in practice as something closer to a standing voice in the room where domestic security policy is formed. His daughter Constance Langford, thirty-two, manages the Foundation's operations and is widely considered the more interesting of the two.

Galmier Family

Nominally a townhouse in lower Aldenmere, functionally the family spends the majority of its time across the archipelago, specifically on Havelock, where the Galmier administrative compound occupies the island's most defensible high point.

The Galmiers hold the Wardenship of the Archipelago, a hereditary administrative title established by royal charter in 1763, when the islands' growing commercial importance made it apparent that governing them through the city's standard municipal framework was producing delays, confusion, and revenue losses that the Dominion could not sustain. The Warden holds authority over the archipelago's taxation, its port regulation, its internal legal proceedings up to a specified severity, and the management of its infrastructure, including the ferry services, the causeway, and the lighthouse network that makes the upper bay navigable in the fog conditions that characterise most of autumn and winter. It has a significant amount of practical authority over a collection of communities that generate a meaningful portion of Vallam's seafood supply, its informal shipping trade, and the particular social ecosystem on the islands, close enough to the great city, but far enough away to seem unique.

Warden Pilar Galmier, fifty-seven, has held the position for nineteen years following her mother’s death and has proved considerably more capable in it than her mother’s associates expected when they first assessed her, an assessment she has not forgiven. She is dark-complexioned, and governs the archipelago with the pragmatic attention to function over form that island administration requires.

Her son Aldric Galmier, twenty-nine, manages the Wardenship's mainland interface from the Aldenmere townhouse and the Merchant District administrative office, intermediating the relationship between the archipelago's commercial interests and the city's commercial interests.

The Cerani Club Organisation

The Cerani Club, Aldenmere, a private club whose public face serves wine to regular members and tells newcomers it does not sell alcohol, this statement being delivered calmly and without apparent irony while wine is visible being consumed at the adjacent tables

The Cerani Club organisation is the oldest substantial criminal enterprise in Vallam, its roots traceable to the early years of Prohibition when a consortium of Cerani-heritage businessmen recognised the opportunity created by the Act's passage and moved quickly to fill the distribution gap in Vallam's wealthiest neighborhoods. At its peak, in 1923–1925, the organisation controlled the majority of alcohol supply into Aldenmere, a significant portion of the upper Merchant District, and a protection arrangement with several Terrace establishments. It held this position for two years before losing most of it.

The event that ended the Cerani Club's dominance, being the killing of its second-in-command (a man known as Clyde Harris) and three senior distribution lieutenants in a series of incidents now referred to, in the criminal community, as the Bad Quarter, was the work of the Vulburrow organisation (see below), which moved with enough speed and enough coordination to significantly disrupt the Cerani Club before it could respond effectively. The Club's current territory is Aldenmere proper and the lower mountain approaches, a position that is comfortable and not expanding. Its leadership knows it is not expanding.

The organisation's effective head is Carlo Benedetti, fifty-five, who manages the Club's legitimate operations. It is a real private members' club with real wine and real food and a real membership, and its criminal ones with an equal hand. He is a Reformist Astralist who attends services at a cathedral in Greenvale monthly and who has, by his own count, not committed a violent act personally since 1918.

He has arranged several since then. He considers the distinction meaningful. His relationship with the Rajiv family is long-standing, mutually beneficial, and does not involve anything written down.

The Corvenza Cartel

The Merchant District, anchored around a trucking and carriage company, Clover Freight, operating from a yard on the district's southern edge

The Corvenza organisation operates Vallam's most comprehensive legitimate-front structure: a trucking and carriage company that genuinely moves goods around the city and its surrounds, a ferry service operating between the mainland and the archipelago, and a marine freight service that runs regular routes between Vallam and several other coastal cities. All of these operations are real, profitable, and useful. They are also the infrastructure through which a significant volume of the city's illicit imports, being alcohol from Răvin, controlled pharmaceuticals from Cerani, goods of various descriptions from several points of origin that the Corvenza Cartel does not advertise, move into and around Vallam.

The cartel is run by the Corvenza family, a Merchant District institution across three generations: the current operational head is Dario Corvenza, forty-eight, whose father established the legitimate freight company in 1897 and whose grandfather was a docker on the same wharf that Dario's ferry service now operates from. Dario Corvenza is efficient, present, and has the authority that comes from running something that genuinely works. The cartel's primary criminal relationship is with the Răvin Workers' Congress's international networks. A supply connection established in the early years of Prohibition that provides a reliable flow of smuggled alcohol and other goods through the Zodiac route, arriving as legitimate cargo and diverging at the Corvenza yards.

Vulburrow Organization

Operating across the Confluence, Ironside, and Fenwick, with no single permanent headquarters. Meetings rotate.

The Vulburrow organisation is the most numerous criminal enterprise in Vallam in 1926, having expanded rapidly through the Prohibition period into most of the city's working-class and middle-class neighborhoods by providing a simpler service than its competitors. Reliable alcohol at reasonable prices, delivered without excessive complexity. Its leadership model is correspondingly flat, with fewer hierarchical levels than the Cerani Club, more operating cells with direct territory responsibility, and a culture that rewards reliability over loyalty in the more elaborate sense.

It is not the most sophisticated operation in Vallam, and it knows it. The men who lead Vulburrow's cells have come up through the same neighborhoods as their customers, and the organisation's relationship with those spots is accordingly more embedded than the Cerani Club's or the Sycamore Room's. It is also more vulnerable. Its flat structure makes it effective at operating but less effective at responding to the kind of top-level strategic pressure that the Sycamore Room is increasingly applying.

The organisation takes its name from its founder, Thomas Vulburrow, who died in 1923 and is remembered within the organisation with reverence. His successor is Delia Marsh, forty-one, a former Millfield clerk who managed Vulburrow's financial operations from the beginning and took over the organisation's leadership after Thomas's death (in favour of his son Jack Vulburrow) simply by being the person who knew where everything was, how everything worked, and what the relevant people owed. The transition was not challenged all too much outside of Jack’s crew, and people who know Delia Marsh tend to understand immediately why.

The Sycamore Room

The Sycamore Room, a three-story underground club beneath the Grand Peeress Hotel on the Terrace. The hotel above it is also owned by the same family.

The Sycamore Room is the newest and most aggressively ambitious criminal organisation in Vallam. It was established approximately four years ago by the Romano family, specifically by Lexy Romano, thirty-seven, and her brother Nico Romano, thirty-four, as the consolidation of several smaller Terrace-area operations into a single entity with unified leadership, unified finances, and a strategy oriented toward long-term dominance rather than the immediate profit that most criminal operations optimise for.

The Romanos came from money. Legitimate, Cerani-origin trade wealth that gave them the initial capital and the social connections to build the Sycamore Room from an actually functioning hotel and nightclub rather than from a back-room arrangement masquerading as one. The club is, by any account, one of the best establishments in Vallam: its performers are exceptional, its wine list is extensive and not fake, and its private rooms, available for the right guests at rates that reflect their privacy rather than their occupancy, are used by people whose presence in the same building as an illegal operation would be legally inconvenient for several of them.

The organisation's members are identifiable to those who know by the purple flower brooch. A stylised sycamore blossom, worn in the lapel, and by the performers whose hair is dyed in the same deep violet.

The Sycamore Room's current operations include a city-wide protection network, the most comprehensive alcohol supply arrangement in the Terrace district, and an expanding network of safehouses and supplier relationships across the surrounding shires that is progressively constricting the other organisations' supply lines. Lexy Romano reads three newspapers every morning and has a map of the city's criminal territory on the wall of her private office that is updated weekly. Nico Romano handles the operations that require physical presence and does not use a map, because he already knows where everything is.

Black Tally

Nominally based in the Confluence and Ironside, with gatherings ( they call them rallies) rotating between three warehouse locations that change on a monthly schedule that members are given at each meeting for the next one only

The Black Tally presents itself, in the portions of its identity it presents publicly, as a veterans' mutual aid society: men who came back from the Corda front changed, who found that civilian life had no adequate framework for what they had seen and done and endured, who organised around shared experience for mutual support and, eventually, for other purposes. This presentation is not entirely false. The men in the Black Tally did come back from the front, they have changed, and the organisation does provide a community for them that they have not found elsewhere.

The Tally is also something else, and something else is not entirely clear to most of its members. Its lower ranks, what its leadership calls the Counts, know the organisation as a bootlegging and robbery operation with an unusual internal culture with rituals, terminology, a hierarchical structure that has a specifically ceremonial quality to it, and reverence for certain objects and certain individuals that goes beyond what most criminal organisations produce in their members. Its higher ranks, referred to internally as the Old Brass, in a usage that sounds like military slang but isn't quite, know more. The full extent of what the Old Brass know is not communicated to the Counts, in the same way that most cults manage the relationship between what the inner circle understands and what the outer membership is told.

Its founding members are veterans who served in the Corda front's longest-running sections. The areas of continuous engagement, the positions that were held for years at a time. They came back at different points and from different units, without obvious prior connection. They found each other in Vallam and formed the organisation in 1921. The organisation's membership has not significantly decreased, which is notable given its involvement in activities that carry significant mortality rates.

The Merr-Boys

The Confluence's eastern edge, centred on an abandoned brickyard off Kiln Street that the gang uses as a base, meeting point, and occasional residence for members who have nowhere else to be

The Merr-Boys are a street gang of subadults, primarily from Ironside and the western Confluence, who have organised around territory, shared identity, and the social infrastructure of a peer group that the city's other institutions have not provided them. The Merr-Boys are not, by Vallam's criminal standards, a significant force, for their activities run to petty theft, minor extortion of local businesses, and the occasional violent confrontation. They hold neutral ground relative to the city's major criminal organisations because they are too small to be worth absorbing and not in anyone's specific territory.

The gang's internal culture centres on a brickyard, which they have been using as a base long enough that several generations of Confluence-area teenagers have passed through it. The current leadership is informal, the Merr-Boys do not have titles, but centres on Finn Merrit, seventeen, whose family name is the probable origin of the gang's name through a linguistic compression that nobody now living can quite reconstruct. The Merr-Boys' ongoing conflict with the Crofts is largely territorial and largely theatrical in the sense that both sides have enough sense not to actually kill anyone.

The Crofts

Ironside's western streets, centred on a boxing gym on Croft Lane, a name that may be the origin of the gang's name or may have been adopted to fit the street; nobody is entirely sure which came first

The Crofts are the Merr-Boys' primary rival and occasional sparring partner in a more literal sense than most gang rivalries. The gym that serves as their base is a functioning boxing gym with actual training equipment, and a significant number of Crofts members have genuine athletic capability that the gang's culture encourages and that has, in several cases, provided individual members with exits from the gang's trajectory into legitimate sporting careers. The Crofts are predominantly of Cerani heritage, second and third generation, and their connection to the Cerani community of Ironside and the Narrows gives them a cultural identity that the Merr-Boys lack. The Crofts' relationship with the Cerani Club organisation is acknowledged within the gang but not formalised, and the Club does not recruit from the Crofts directly, but several former Crofts members have ended up in the Club's lower-tier operations, and the Club is aware of and comfortable with the Crofts' presence as a territorial stabilising factor in Ironside's western streets.
