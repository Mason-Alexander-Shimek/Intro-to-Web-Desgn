The Vallam Metropolitan Police Department

Central Precinct
The Sovereign Quarter

The Vallam Metropolitan Police Department (VMPD) is divided into eight precincts loosely aligned with the city's major neighborhoods. The Central Precinct, located in the Sovereign Quarter, serves as headquarters. Commissioner Arthur Voss, a career administrator, has managed the department through Prohibition by maintaining a careful distinction between what he knows and what he officially chooses to ignore.

The VMPD's relationship with Vallam's criminal underworld varies by precinct, ranging from active enforcement cooperation to near-total institutional capture. The Prohibition Division operates within the VMPD's structure but reports directly to the national Home Office, creating resentment throughout the regular force. Relations between the two bodies are strained, and inter-agency cooperation is minimal.

The Prohibition Division

Location
Millfield, a deliberately unremarkable building on Crane Street

Formed under the National Prohibition Act of 1924, the Prohibition Division enforces national alcohol restrictions as the enforcement arm of the Temperance Bureau. Its officers are recruited primarily from outside the regular police force, which was considered too compromised to enforce Prohibition reliably. The Division excels at high-profile raids, prominent bootlegger arrests, and major shipment seizures but struggles with routine enforcement due to its officers' lack of established neighborhood relationships.

The Division is headed by Chief Agent Emmeline Strauch, a former customs investigator known for her competence and blunt manner. Recognizing that the VMPD ranges from indifferent to openly hostile toward her mandate, she has developed independent networks of informants and contacts outside its structure. Commissioner Voss finds these arrangements deeply irritating.

The Suppression Bureau

Location
Officially listed as a division of the Home Ministry, physically located in a four-story building in the Sovereign Quarter that is not labeled and has no public-facing signage beyond a street number.

The Suppression Bureau operates covertly within the Dominion government under the official designation Office for Cultural and Historical Integrity, a deliberately vague title that has concealed its true mandate for thirty years. The Bureau manages the boundary between the ordinary world and the supernatural, restricting exposure to phenomena and knowledge that can endanger those who encounter them.

Its primary functions include: the censorship and removal of written, artistic, and printed works that describe, reference, or inadvertently replicate knowledge of the horrors; the investigation and, if necessary, the disappearance of individuals who have come into contact with supernatural entities or knowledge and represent a risk of further exposure or contamination; the monitoring of organisations and individuals with demonstrated occult interest; and the containment of what the Bureau's internal documents call threshold events. These refer to occurrences in which the boundary between the ordinary and the supernatural has been breached in ways that require immediate and deniable management.

The Bureau's agents carry badges whose statement reads Aliis Si Licet, Tibi Non Licet, which can translate to “What is permitted for others is not permitted for you”, a quotation selected by the Bureau's founder. Agents receive only enough information to perform their assigned duties and are discouraged from investigating the broader mandate. The Bureau maintains that restricted knowledge protects agents from psychological harm, a rationale that is partly justified and partly convenient.

Director Zachary Wakefield, forty-one, heads the Vallam Station. His background before joining the Bureau is undocumented in any files accessible to his agents. He rarely leaves the Sovereign Quarter building; when he does, observers take notice and question what prompted his departure. The Bureau cooperates with St. Butcher's Custos when operationally necessary and surveys the Hush House, the University's Society for the Exploration of the Unexplained, and several individuals connected to the Eclipsiad's surviving underground presence. People disappear from Vallam regularly, and some would likely remain had the Bureau not needed them to.

The Dominion Naval Command

Location
A large complex on the Merchant District's northern waterfront, adjacent to the naval dockyards.

Headquartered at a large complex on the Merchant District's northern waterfront, adjacent to the naval dockyards. The Navy is present in Vallam as a major institutional fact of the city's life. Its dockyards employ several thousand civilian workers, its officers occupy a significant portion of the Aldenmere lower-slope residences, and its command structure is one of the city's most important centres of power. Admiral Constance Harrow, the first woman to hold the position, appointed in 1923 after a career that included the most successful anti-smuggling operation in Dominion naval history and the management of the Selkeff Straits monitoring station during the early war years, is one of the most significant people in Vallam and one of the least publicly prominent, which she considers an appropriate arrangement.

The Vallam Standard

A six-story building in Millfield, whose printing presses are audible from the street during night runs.

The Standard is the Dominion's newspaper of record. The broadsheet that foreign governments read to understand what Fairbourne thinks, that Parliament reads to understand what the public thinks, and that the public reads to understand what Parliament is doing wrong. It has been publishing continuously since 1801 and has, over that period, broken more significant stories than any other paper on the Reach, retracted fewer of them than any reasonable person would expect, and been the target of more legal threats than all other Vallam newspapers combined.

Its editor-in-chief, Barnabas Quelch, is seventy-one, has been at the Standard for forty-three years, holds views that cannot be mapped onto any recognisable political position without significant distortion, and has an editorial instinct that his reporters find terrifying and his proprietors find, with some justice, more valuable than the building they publish from. The Standard's relationship with the Suppression Bureau is contentious. The Bureau has successfully suppressed four Standard stories in the last decade. The Standard is, in all four cases, aware that the suppression occurred.

The Vallam Evening Courier

Location
The Narrows, Southgate Building

The Standard's principal competitor for serious news coverage, younger by forty years, considerably more willing to pursue stories through methods the Standard's code of ethics does not endorse, and consistently better at the kind of crime reporting that requires sources in the criminal community.

Its crime desk is staffed primarily by former police officers and one former bootlegger whose arrangement with the Courier's editor has never been formally documented. Its editor, Petra Daun, is forty-four, was the Courier's crime reporter for fifteen years before being given the chair, and has a network of informants across Vallam's underworld that several law enforcement agencies would describe as inappropriately comprehensive if they were willing to acknowledge officially that they know about it.

The Morning Post

Headquartered in ashford, a functional brick building on the high street

The Morning Post is not the Standard or the Courier. It knows it is not the Standard or the Courier, and it has made a consistent editorial decision that the correct response to this is to be more useful to the people who actually read it rather than to the people who do not. It covers Vallam's neighborhoods with greater granularity than either of its larger competitors. Local council votes, school board appointments, street-level crime, the kind of community news that the Standard considers beneath notice and the Courier considers insufficiently dramatic. Its readership is Ashford and Ironside and Fenwick. Its journalists are from those neighborhoods and know them in ways that no desk at Millfield or the Narrows can replicate. It breaks local stories that the Standard then follows up on at three times the space and half the accuracy.

Illustrated Gazette

Situated in Greenvale near the university

A weekly periodical combining long-form journalism, cultural criticism, and illustration. Its arts coverage is the best in the city. Its political coverage takes the form of long essays that are read by the people who write them, the people who disagree with them, and not many others. Its editor, Marguerite Palliser, is a former academic who treats the Gazette as an ongoing argument with the city about what matters, and whose back-page column, weekly, unsigned, and written in a style that makes it instantly identifiable, has been both prosecuted for libel twice and nominated for the Dominion's press award four times. She lost both trials and won two nominations

Vallam Chamber of Commerce

Location
Millfield, Commerce House, a large Edwardian building with a public meeting room that can seat three hundred.

The Chamber is the city's primary business advocacy body, formally voluntary, practically indispensable for any commercial operation that wants a voice in city policy, licensing decisions, or the various municipal processes that govern commercial life in Vallam. Its membership ranges from the large Aldenmere holding companies to the shopkeepers of Coppergate, though the Chamber's governance structure, a board elected by members weighted by contribution tier, means that in practice the large interests tend to set the agenda and the small ones attend the quarterly meetings and vote accordingly.

The current Chamber president is Duncan Crayle, the managing director of a mid-sized shipping insurance firm whose career has been built on knowing more about who needs what than anyone else in the room. Crayle is not corrupt but deeply well-informed, which produces similar outcomes in many situations. The Chamber's relationship with the city's criminal enterprises is mostly transactional and mostly conducted at arm's length. A number of the Chamber's members operate legitimate businesses that are adjacent to illegitimate supply chains, and the Chamber has a consistent institutional policy of not asking questions whose answers would require it to do something.

Rotary Club

Location
Ashford, meeting weekly at the Ashford Arms public house (currently serving what the landlord describes as a selection of non-alcoholic fermented beverages)

Vallam's Rotary chapter was founded in 1912 by a group of younger professionals who found the Chamber of Commerce too formal and the established clubs too expensive, and wanted an organization focused on doing something useful rather than simply knowing useful people. The current membership is largely drawn from the working professional class, teachers, junior lawyers, small business owners, mid-grade civil servants, with a higher proportion of women than any of the other civic organisations and a younger average age than all of them.

The Rotary's service projects in 1926 include a reading program in Ironside's primary schools, a weekly soup provision operation in Coppergate, and an ongoing campaign to get the city council to finally address Fenwick's drainage problem. None of these projects are glamorous, but they are all doing something. The club's president, Vera Ashby, is a secondary school teacher at Ashford Comprehensive who runs the organisation with a cheerful relentlessness that its members find either inspiring or exhausting depending on the week and how many service hours they have had to log.

The Masonic Lodge. Charter No. 7

Location
Millfield, a building on Redfield Street that is distinguished from its neighbors primarily by the absence of any signage whatsoever.

The Masonic Lodge has been operating in Vallam since 1788, long enough that its membership rolls read as a partial history of the city's professional and academic establishment. Its rituals, so far as the public knows anything about them, involve a mixture of ceremonial tradition, fraternal obligation, and a system of moral philosophy expressed through symbolic architecture that members take more seriously than outsiders tend to expect.

Its secrets, which are genuinely kept, are less dramatic than popular imagination suggests and more practical. The lodge's membership overlaps extensively with the city's judiciary, its senior civil service, and its major financial institutions, and the fraternal obligation of the lodge produces a network of informal professional support and mutual assistance that operates quietly and effectively. The Master of the Lodge is Sir Edmund Bracke, a retired judge whose post-retirement social life is conducted almost entirely through the lodge's structures.

The Companion Circle, an informal associate group for women, acknowledged but formally separate from the lodge, meets monthly in a private room at the Grand Peeress Hotel. It does not include the lodge's ritual elements and has a considerably more overtly social character. Several of its members have been attempting for ten years to formalize the association into something with actual lodge status. This effort is proceeding in the way that such efforts proceed inside institutions that would prefer them not to succeed.

Vallam Athletic Association

Location
A large Victorian building in Greenvale, adjacent to the University sports grounds, known simply as the VAA House.

The VAA is the city's central sports and socialising club for those who want the social infrastructure of an athletic association without the commitment to regular athletic activity. Its membership includes genuine sportspeople, such as the the Rowing Club, the Amateur Boxing League, and a Cricket Association that takes itself seriously, plus a substantially larger contingent who joined for the discounted bar, the connections, and the general pleasure of belonging to something whose atmosphere smells of liniment and old leather without having to exert themselves. Both constituencies are welcome, and the VAA's success as an institution derives largely from this inclusive approach.

Its facilities include a gymnasium, a swimming pool, several dining rooms, a bar, a billiard hall, and a room informally designated as the smoking lounge in which no smoking has technically been possible since the incident of 1919, the nature of which is not recorded in the official minutes. The President, Captain (Ret.) Frederick Orme, is a former army officer whose definition of sport expands conveniently to include most of what the VAA's members actually want to do there.

The Society for the Exploration of the Unexplained

Location
University of Vallam, a borrowed basement room beneath the Esoteric Studies building, meetings Wednesday evenings

The SEU began in 1919 as a response to a wave of interest among returning veterans and their younger university counterparts in ghost stories, paranormal reports from the front, and the general sense that the world, having demonstrated its willingness to be much stranger and more horrible than expected, might also be strange in more interesting ways. Its membership is drawn from the University's more socially peripheral students. The ones who find the mainstream student social world slightly impenetrable and prefer a context in which enthusiasm for strange subjects is a social asset rather than a liability.

The SEU investigates reported paranormal events in Vallam with the rigour available to a group of undergraduates who have read a lot but have little field experience. Their investigations are almost uniformly unsuccessful in finding genuine evidence of anything supernatural, which is partly because the city's actual supernatural activity is well enough concealed that undergraduate investigators won't stumble onto it by accident, and partly because the SEU's investigative methodology, while enthusiastic, is not sophisticated. Several of its members are, however, more observant than average, and the SEU's archive of reported strange occurrences in Vallam, five years of case files, compiled with inconsistent but genuine diligence, contains, buried in the less dramatic material, several reports that the Suppression Bureau has reason to wish it had found before the SEU did.

The faculty advisor, Dr. Clement Foss, teaches in the Esoteric Studies faculty and is broadly supportive of the SEU in the sense that he signs the paperwork required for them to exist as a university society and does not attend their meetings. He is aware of considerably more than the SEU's members and has made a careful decision about how much of that awareness to share.

The Vallam Ladies' Shooting Club

University of Vallam, the Pennant Building, with a fully soundproofed firing range in the basement

Officially registered as a marksmanship sports society under the University's athletics programme, the VLSC is, in practice, a government-sponsored recruitment pipeline for the Dominion's Women's Auxiliary Corps, which has been accepting female volunteers into non-combat military roles since 1917 and combat-adjacent roles since 1922, and which has been trying to do both of these things without making it obviously a military recruitment operation since a Public Conscription Debate in 1924. The Club's official patron is the University's Athletics Department. Its practical administration is managed by a liaison from the Dominion War Ministry whose university affiliation is described, on all relevant paperwork, as visiting instructor in applied athletics.

The Club's membership includes women who genuinely want to learn to shoot, women who have intuited or been quietly told about the military connection and want to join the war effort, women who think it is funny that the Dominion's military recruitment is happening in a university shooting club basement, and one woman, Sergeant Instructor Ilara Vetch (university paperwork: Miss Vetch, Instructor), who has been running the Club since 1921 and whose combat record from the Corda front is significantly more extensive than her title suggests. The firing range is in use most evenings and all day Saturday and Sunday. The walls are thick enough to be effectively soundproofed.

Sergeant-Instructor Vetch has made this point to University administration on several occasions when other building use has been proposed.

The St. Aldrek's Club

A converted townhouse on Fenwick's main residential street, three buildings from the St. Aldrek's College eastern gate

The St. Aldrek's Club is, formally, a private members' club for students, alumni and senior staff of St.

Aldrek's College, offering dining, reading rooms, overnight accommodation for visiting alumni, and the specific institutional feeling of a place whose purpose is to allow its members to be unavailable. Its building has been modified over the decades to eliminate window sight lines from the street, to install a rear entrance accessible without passing through the main lobby, and to soundproof, with considerable thoroughness, its private dining rooms.

These modifications were funded by the Club's membership and are not mentioned in its official prospectus. Within the Club's structure, the most significant and least officially acknowledged entity is the Aldrek Skulls, the Club's senior fraternity, whose membership is invitation-only, whose meetings are conducted in a room that non-members do not know exists, and whose influence in the placement of St. Aldrek's graduates into positions in law, finance, and the civil service has been the subject of four separate Standard investigations, none of which produced enough documentation to publish. The Skulls are not sinister in any supernatural sense. They are sinister in the ordinary sense of being a group of men with institutional connections and significant mutual interest in each other's continued success, operating without accountability.

The Skulls' current chair is Reginald Vacarro, a property lawyer whose office in Millfield represents a portfolio of clients whose legal needs occasionally require the kind of discretion that the Skulls' network is specifically equipped to provide.

Vallam Amateur Theatre Company

The Terrace, a converted former music hall called The Lamp Room, leased from the city at rates that the City Council reviews annually and consistently fails to raise

The VATC is one of Vallam's oldest continuously operating amateur artistic institutions, founded in 1887 by a group of civil servants who found that government work, while intellectually satisfying, offered insufficient opportunity for melodrama. It has since become the primary social institution for a particular cross-section of the city: people who work in professional occupations during the day and want, in the evenings, to be someone else entirely. Its membership includes accountants, lawyers, teachers, a surprising number of police officers, and at least three individuals whose day occupation is of sufficient sensitivity that their VATC membership is the main thing their colleagues know about their personal lives.

The Company produces four shows per year from a mixture of established works, original productions, and the occasional experiment that the Committee describes as ambitious and the audience describes as testing, in The Lamp Room's intimate 180-seat house. Backstage at The Lamp Room is one of the more reliable social environments in Vallam. The greenroom is never empty, the caretaker serves tea at all hours without being asked, and the Company's tradition of post-show suppers at the restaurant next door has continued without interruption since 1891, including during the recent years of the Prohibition Act during which the restaurant's wine list became, officially, a menu of grape-based restorative beverages.

The Company's director is Sylvester Quine, a former professional actor who had a respectable provincial career before returning to Vallam and discovering that he preferred directing amateurs to performing with professionals, because amateurs are doing it because they love it, and that turns out to matter. He is also one of the more reliable sources in the city for information about who is having an affair with whom, a fact that several parties value for different reasons.

First Ladies of Vallam

Nominally, wherever they happen to be. In practice, The Terrace hotels, the Confluence clubs, the speakeasies of the Narrows, and various private residences across Aldenmere

The First Ladies are not exactly a formal organisation. They are a social network with an identity, defined by shared ancestry, shared wealth, and a shared decision that the constraints society places on women of their position are less binding than those women are generally led to believe. Membership requires either a demonstrable Vallam lineage traceable to colonial-era settlement, a documented family presence in the city of three or more generations, or sufficient wealth and the endorsement of three existing members, which in practice means that the society is roughly equally divided between old money who earned their membership through birth and new money who earned it through the right combination of charming the right people.

The First Ladies have a reputation in Vallam that they are “aware of, have largely decided to cultivate, and consider to be, on balance, more accurate than their critics would prefer to acknowledge”. They are louder, more present, and more comfortable in spaces traditionally dominated by men than the social conventions of 1926 technically endorse. Several of them are genuinely talented musicians and performers; the nucleus of the group grew from a shared interest in jazz and the social world that surrounded it, and that connection to the music and the clubs remains central to the First Ladies' character.

Their informal leader (the First Ladies have no formal leadership, but there is a person whose preferences shape what the group does) is Adeline Fairchild, twenty-eight, a member of the Fairchild branch of the Fairbourne family offshoot network, who plays the piano with a technical accomplishment she consistently refuses to take seriously and whose social influence extends far further than she typically allows people to know she is exerting it.

Hush House

Strand Isle, in a large Victorian manor known as Hush House, accessible by private ferry from the southern Merchant District docks

The Hush House Society presents itself, to the limited extent that it presents itself to anything outside its membership, as a society devoted to esoteric philosophy, alchemical study, and the collection of rare books. This presentation is accurate. It is also incomplete.

The Society was founded in 1889 by Desmond Farrant, an independently wealthy antiquarian whose collection of pre-Astralist texts represented, at the time of his death in 1911, one of the most comprehensive private occult libraries on the Reach. The manor he used as its headquarters, Hush House, on Strand Isle, a building whose peculiar acoustic qualities give it a permanent hushed quality that visitors invariably note and cannot explain, passed to the Society rather than to Farrant's family, a provision of his will that his family contested unsuccessfully for several years.

The current Society numbers thirty-one members, drawn from Vallam's professional and artistic upper-middle class. Most of them understand themselves to be members of a sophisticated intellectual club with excellent library access and a pleasingly theatrical approach to its own meetings. Some of them have noticed that the library's deeper stacks contain texts that are not catalogued and that produce, in extended reading, a specific kind of headache and a tendency to see things in peripheral vision. A very small number have noticed that the headache is not the worst thing the texts can produce.

The Society's current chair is Theodora Clemens, a retired chemist who joined in 1914 and has spent the twelve years since moving carefully and methodically through the library's uncatalogued section, recording what she finds, and reaching conclusions that she has not shared with any of the Society's other members. She is sixty-seven, moves slowly, asks precise questions, and has not been unwell in fourteen years, which she attributes to a good diet.

The Vallam Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals

Greenvale, a converted Victorian house three blocks from the University hospital, with a large garden that has been modified to serve as an animal welfare facility

The VSPCA was founded in 1891 and has operated as an entirely conventional animal welfare organisation for most of its history, running Vallam's only animal shelter, advocating for animal welfare legislation, and prosecuting cruelty cases through civil channels. Its current membership is approximately two hundred, with a core volunteer group of around thirty who do the actual work.

The organisation's unofficial mascot and most significant asset, in terms of public attention, is a family of five red pandas that arrived in Vallam in 1921 aboard a diplomatic vessel from a Renascia-aligned state, as part of a gift shipment to a private collector who subsequently went bankrupt and lost them to creditors. The VSPCA acquired them through a city auction, built a suitable enclosure in its garden, and discovered that the public's appetite for visiting the organisation's premises increased by approximately 400% immediately thereafter. The eldest and largest of the five, a round, placid animal named Cohh who has achieved the status of local celebrity, is the most photographed resident of Vallam after the King and is generally acknowledged by the organisation's members to be responsible for a significant percentage of its annual donations.

Vallam Gun Club

Below the Aldenmere Golf Course, on the mountain's east face, in a long timber-and-stone building accessible by a road that is not on any city map and which gun club members simply know

The Vallam Gun Club occupies a position in Aldenmere's social ecosystem somewhere between a sporting society and a private members' club that has chosen firearms as its organising rationale. Its membership is drawn from the Aldenmere wealthy with a minority of enthusiasts from Greenvale and Millfield who have been admitted through the standard introduction process and who have learned that the Gun Club's social functions, monthly dinners, annual shooting competition (whose prizes are disproportionate to the competition's actual prestige), and informal gatherings on the practice range that last longer than practice ranges ordinarily warrant, are its primary purpose and the shooting is the justification.

The Club is owned and operated by Horatio Parrington, the proprietor of Parrington's on Ironside Street, Vallam's most comprehensively stocked firearms retailer, which serves both the professional and the sporting market with equal accommodation. Parrington is sixty-three, compact, with the specific physique of someone who has handled precision instruments all his life, and possesses a conversational style that produces, in people who underestimate him, the gradual discovery that they have told him considerably more than they intended to. He is not affiliated with any criminal organisation and knows about most of them, because they buy from him, and Parrington has a policy of excellent memory and no questions.

The Ecdysis Society

The Narrows, in a building that is not findable without knowing which door to push and which painted number to ignore.

Opens only on nights of the full moon.

The Ecdysis Society is a performance and dance collective that is, in the city's broader cultural consciousness, simultaneously famous and impossible to describe accurately to anyone who hasn't been. It is known (imprecisely) through the accounts of those who have attended, as a dance club, a performance space, a sensory experience, something to do with transformation, something to do with shedding, something that you are told about by a friend who attended and who struggles to explain exactly what they saw, only that they watched it for what felt like twenty minutes and left at three in the morning.

It is run by Amavasya Hessenger, a woman whose background is not clearly established in any source that anyone has been able to verify. She is tall and bright-eyed in a way that suggests either exceptional health or the permanent presence of something being very interested in everything around her. She blinks infrequently. The Society's logo on the door, membership cards and brooches worn by the performers is a moth. The performers of the Ecdysis Society are extraordinary. What they do varies each month, shaped by Hessenger's direction and the dynamic of the audience and performers present.

St. Butcher's Cathedral (The Main Congregation)

Ironside, occupying a full city block on the neighborhood's central street

St. Butcher's is Vallam's largest and most prominent Lysterist cathedral, the seat of the Bishop of Vallam and the primary place of worship for the city's Lysterist majority. Its choir school, its charitable programmes, and its social presence in Ironside and the surrounding neighborhoods make it one of the city's significant civic institutions in addition to its religious function. The Bishop, Aldous Maren, is seventy-six, scholarly, theologically sophisticated, and genuinely uncertain about several things that his position officially requires certainty about. He is also one of the more perceptive readers of character in Vallam, which makes him interesting to a wider range of people than his age and position might suggest.

St. Butcher's Custos

St. Butcher's Cathedral, operating from the chapter house's lower level, which has two rooms that are kept locked and are not on the cathedral's floor plan

The Custos is publicly described as a Classical Astralist outreach ministry of St. Butcher's, focused on practical community service and pastoral care across Vallam's neighborhoods.

The Custos was established in 1847 following an event in the Narrows that the official cathedral record describes as “an episode of civil disorder requiring pastoral response” and that Custos internal records describe in considerably more specific and alarming terms. Since then, the Custos has operated as what is essentially an ecclesiastical rapid response capability for supernatural incursion: a small group of trained individuals, drawn primarily from the cathedral's clergy and lay members, who are aware of the nature of the world's darker inhabitants and who maintain a body of procedural knowledge, about how to manage the consequences of encounters with them.

The Custos's training includes practical components that would surprise the cathedral's general congregation, and its small library includes texts that the Suppression Bureau is intermittently interested in and consistently unable to remove, partly because removing them from church property raises jurisdictional issues and partly because the Custos's senior membership tends to inexplicably know they're coming.

The Custos operates approximately twenty active members in 1926, organised in small response teams under the leadership of Father Billy Callum, a former military chaplain whose tour in the Corda campaign exposed him to things that field chaplaincy training had not prepared him for and that led, through a sequence of events he does not enjoy describing, to his current position. He is forty-four and has a relationship with the Suppression Bureau that is best described as mutually reluctant professional cooperation.
